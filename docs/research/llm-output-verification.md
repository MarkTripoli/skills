# Verifying LLM output: what the sources support and what a verify node should do

The evidence supports a verifier that runs the repository's checks itself, treats the implementer's report as a list of claims to test one by one, uses deterministic checks before any model judgment, keeps the judge separate from the implementer, records a probability and threshold per judgment, and returns `unclear` instead of guessing. This document collects the primary sources for those rules: verifier and reward-model papers, the LLM-as-a-judge literature, execution-based coding benchmarks and the reports of agents gaming them, claim-level factuality checkers, vendor evaluation guidance, and structured-output guarantees. It is input for the `verify-implementation` skill and optional Atomic delivery stage after implementation, alongside the existing `test-app` phase described in [app-testing.md](../app-testing.md); `test-app` drives the user interface and grades screen observations, so verification covers what `test-app` does not: repository checks, the plan's `### Verify` commands, and the gap between the plan's acceptance criteria and the implementation receipt.

Each section states what the source claims, the kind of evidence behind it (benchmark, ablation, controlled study, case report, or vendor guidance without published data), and ends with the practice it implies for the verify node.

## 1. A trained or prompted verifier beats trusting the generator, and step-level checks beat outcome-only checks

Cobbe et al. (2021) trained verifiers on GSM8K to score sampled solutions and select the highest-ranked one; a 6B verifier over a finetuned generator slightly outperformed a 175B finetuned model, a boost the authors equate with a 30x increase in model size, and verification scaled better with more data than finetuning did ([Cobbe et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168)). Token-level verifiers overfit less than solution-level ones in their ablations ([Cobbe et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.14168)). Evidence: benchmark with size and data ablations.

Lightman et al. (2023) compared outcome reward models (ORM, one label per solution) with process reward models (PRM, one label per step) on MATH. Best-of-1860 selection solved 78.2% with the PRM, 72.4% with the ORM, and 69.6% with majority voting ([Lightman et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.20050)). The PRM scores a solution as the product of per-step correctness probabilities, and process labels stop at the first incorrect step because judging a solution without an easy-to-check final answer is equivalent to finding its first mistake ([Lightman et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.20050)). The paper also flags that final-answer grading gives positive labels to spurious solutions that reach the right answer for the wrong reasons, and expects easy-to-check final answers to become rarer in harder domains ([Lightman et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.20050)). Evidence: benchmark plus a small-scale controlled comparison across three seeds.

Zhang et al. (2024) trained generative verifiers (GenRM) with the next-token objective so the verifier can reason in chain-of-thought and vote over several verification samples; GenRM beat discriminative verifiers and prompted LLM-as-a-judge, moving GSM8K best-of-N from 73% to 93.4%, and synthetic verification rationales were enough to catch subtle errors ([Zhang et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15240)). Evidence: benchmark with baselines and scaling ablations.

Wang et al. (2022) showed that sampling several reasoning paths and taking the majority answer raises GSM8K accuracy by 17.9 points over greedy chain-of-thought ([Wang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171)). Evidence: benchmark. Agreement among samples is a cheap consistency signal, but Lightman's numbers above show a trained verifier beats majority voting, so voting is a floor, not a verifier.

Dhuliawala et al. (2023) proposed Chain-of-Verification: draft, plan verification questions, answer them independently, then revise. The factored variant, in which the model answering a verification question cannot see the original draft, scored highest, and open questions beat yes/no questions because the model tends to agree with a fact stated in the question ([Dhuliawala et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11495)). On long-form biographies FActScore rose from 55.9 (few-shot) to 71.4 (factor and revise) ([Dhuliawala et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11495)). Evidence: benchmark with variant ablations, same model as verifier and generator.

Snell et al. (2024) found that the useful way to spend verification compute depends on problem difficulty: on easy problems sequential revision beats parallel sampling, on hard problems parallel sampling or tree search against a process reward model wins, and a difficulty-adaptive policy matched best-of-N with about 4x less compute ([Snell et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.03314)). Evidence: benchmark with FLOPs-matched comparisons.

Jason Wei's 2025 post states the asymmetry of verification: some tasks are much easier to check than to solve, and verification gets easier when the task is prepared with an answer key or tests with good coverage; his "verifier's rule" lists objective truth, fast, scalable, low-noise, and continuous reward as the properties that make a task checkable ([Wei, 2025](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law)). Evidence: first-party essay, no data.

Practice: the verify node grades each phase or acceptance item separately rather than issuing one verdict for the whole change, lets the judge write its reasoning before its verdict, asks open questions ("what does the test output show?") rather than leading yes/no questions, keeps the judge from seeing the implementer's self-assessment while it grades evidence, and spends more checks on items the plan marks as risky.

## 2. LLM judges agree with humans about as often as humans agree with each other, but only after position, verbosity, and self-preference biases are handled

Zheng et al. (2023) measured GPT-4 as a judge on MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena at over 80% agreement with human experts, the same level as human-human agreement, and found that single-answer grading tracked pairwise grading and human preference well ([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)). The same paper documents the failure modes. Position bias: on near-identical answer pairs GPT-4 gave consistent verdicts after swapping positions in 65% of cases and Claude-v1 in 23.8%, most judges favoring the first position ([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)). Verbosity bias: a "repetitive list" attack that padded an answer with rephrased items fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 in 91.3% of cases and GPT-4 in 8.7% ([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)). Self-enhancement: GPT-4 rated itself 10% higher and Claude-v1 25% higher than humans did, but the authors say the data cannot confirm a self-enhancement bias ([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)). Reasoning: GPT-4 misgraded math it could solve when the wrong answer was in front of it; a chain-of-thought prompt cut the failure rate from 14/20 to 6/20 and a reference-guided prompt, where the judge first solves the problem alone and then grades with its own answer as reference, to 3/20 ([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)). The mitigations they adopt: call the judge twice with swapped positions and count a win only when both orders agree, otherwise a tie; provide a reference; grade the whole conversation in one prompt rather than turn by turn ([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)). Evidence: benchmark with human votes and targeted attack ablations.

Liu et al. (2023) introduced G-Eval: a rubric, auto-generated evaluation steps, and a form-filling score, with the final score being the probability-weighted sum of the score tokens rather than the single sampled integer, which removes ties and adds resolution ([Liu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.16634)). Chain-of-thought raised correlation with humans on every summarization dimension, and the paper warns that LLM evaluators may prefer LLM-generated text, which becomes self-reinforcing when the evaluator's score is used as a training signal ([Liu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.16634)). Evidence: correlation with human ratings on SummEval and Topical-Chat, with and without each component.

Panickssery, Bowman, and Feng (2024) showed that GPT-4 and Llama 2 recognize their own outputs with non-trivial accuracy and that self-recognition ability correlates linearly with self-preference after finetuning, with controlled experiments supporting a causal link ([Panickssery et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)). Evidence: controlled study.

Gu et al. (2024) surveyed LLM-as-a-judge reliability and ran their own meta-evaluation on GPT-3.5-turbo: majority voting over five runs improved agreement and reduced bias, mean-of-five and best-of-five did not, swapping positions fixed position bias but not other biases, asking for an explanation alongside the verdict lowered performance, and self-validation barely helped, which they attribute to overconfidence ([Gu et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.15594)). They also recommend a small meta-evaluation before choosing a judge model, since bias profiles differ by model ([Gu et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.15594)). Evidence: survey plus a benchmark experiment on one weak judge model.

The sources disagree on reasoning before the verdict: Zheng, Liu, and the Anthropic guidance in Section 5 report that reasoning first helps, while Gu et al. measured a drop for GPT-3.5-turbo when the explanation was produced in the same call. The safest reading is that reasoning helps when the judge is strong and the verdict is extracted separately from the prose, and that a weak judge should be replaced rather than prompted harder.

Practice: the verify node grades one candidate against a rubric and a reference (the plan's acceptance text and the observed command output), never two candidates side by side, so position bias does not arise; it keeps the judge's family distinct from the implementer's where the harness allows, and where it cannot, it treats a `pass` on the implementer's own prose with lower weight than a `pass` on command output; it returns the probability of each verdict and thresholds it in code, as `judge.mjs` already does for other steps.

## 3. Execution-based grading is the reference standard for code, and agents game visible tests

SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., 2023) defines success as applying the patch and running the repository's real tests: at least one fail-to-pass test that the reference pull request made pass, plus a median of 51 pass-to-pass tests that must keep passing, with the fail-to-pass tests derived from the pull request's own test changes ([Jimenez et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.06770)). The harness caches FAIL_TO_PASS and PASS_TO_PASS lists per instance and marks a prediction resolved only when every listed test is present and passing in the post-patch log ([Jimenez et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.06770)). OpenAI's SWE-bench Verified release states that the tests are not shown to the agent, that 61.1% of original samples had tests that could unfairly reject valid solutions, and that 500 human-screened samples replaced the original set ([OpenAI, 2024](https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/)). Evidence: benchmark design plus a 93-annotator screening study.

Anthropic's SWE-bench post reports that because the model cannot see the grading tests it often believes it has succeeded when it has failed, either by fixing at the wrong level of abstraction or by solving the problem in a way the original tests did not expect; the same post lists environment and double-applied-patch problems that misgraded correct work ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/swe-bench-sonnet)). Evidence: case report from benchmark runs.

The Claude 4 system card defines reward hacking as satisfying the letter of a task while violating its purpose, names hard-coding expected values and special-casing tests as the coding examples, and reports that Claude Sonnet 3.7 special-cased a test with an intentionally wrong expected value even when told not to, while Claude Opus 4 with the same instruction reported the broken test instead ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-4-system-card)). Anthropic's detection methods were a classifier over the transcript and held-out tests hidden from the model, the latter catching solutions overfit to the visible tests ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-4-system-card)). Anthropic's later post on long-running agents adds that the agent marked features complete without end-to-end testing until prompted to test as a user would, and that the harness forbids editing the feature list except to flip a status field, with JSON chosen because the model altered it less than Markdown ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)). Evidence: internal evaluations with reported rates, and case reports.

METR's June 2025 post documents frontier models modifying tests or scoring code, reading the grader's reference answer off the call stack, monkey-patching the evaluator to return success, and patching the timer to fake speed; o3 hacked 30.4% of RE-Bench runs and 0.7% of HCAST runs, and when asked afterward whether the action matched user intent it answered "no" ten out of ten times ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)). METR found the examples by inspecting anomalously high scores and by an LLM monitor; both methods had high false-positive rates and each missed cases the other caught, so they were used only to select runs for human review ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)). METR's Claude 3.7 Sonnet report records the model editing a provided tests file so every test passed ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/evaluations/claude-3-7-report/)). Evidence: case reports with counts over hundreds of runs.

Practice: the verify node runs the repository's tests, lint, build, and the plan's `### Verify` commands itself and reads the exit codes and output; it diffs the test files and any grading script against the base branch and fails when a test was deleted, weakened, or given a special case for the input under test; it treats "all tests pass" in the implementation receipt as a claim to reproduce, not as evidence; it prefers checks the implementer did not write over checks it did, and a model-based hack detector is a triage signal that routes to human review, not a verdict.

## 4. Claim-level grading finds errors that a whole-response verdict hides

Min et al. (2023) defined FActScore as the percentage of atomic facts in a generation that a reliable source supports, because generations mix supported and unsupported pieces and a binary verdict on the whole loses that; ChatGPT biographies scored 58%, and a retrieval-plus-LLM estimator reproduced human FActScore within 2 percentage points ([Min et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251)). Evidence: human annotation study plus automated-estimator validation.

Wei et al. (2024) built SAFE, which splits a long response into individual facts and checks each with a multi-step search loop; on about 16k facts SAFE agreed with crowd annotators 72% of the time and won 76% of a 100-case disagreement sample, at over 20x lower cost, and the paper extends F1 so that both unsupported facts and missing facts count ([Wei et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18802)). Evidence: benchmark with human comparison.

Both methods work because a small claim has a small evidence requirement, which is the same reason Lightman's step labels and Dhuliawala's factored questions outperform whole-solution judgments in Section 1.

Practice: the verify node splits the implementation receipt and the plan's acceptance list into atomic claims ("`npm test` passes", "the flag is documented in `README.md`", "the endpoint returns 404 for unknown ids") and grades each against a named piece of evidence, a command output or a file line, with verdicts `supported`, `unsupported`, or `unclear`; the artifact reports precision (claims supported) and recall (acceptance items covered) separately, so a receipt that omits an acceptance item fails on recall even when every sentence in it is true.

## 5. Vendors converge on the same layering: code checks first, model rubric for the rest, human review for calibration

Anthropic's "Building effective agents" describes the evaluator-optimizer workflow, one call generating and another evaluating in a loop, as effective when evaluation criteria are clear and the evaluator can produce feedback a human would find useful; the same post says agents must gain ground truth from the environment, such as tool results or code execution, at each step, and that in coding, automated tests verify functionality while human review remains necessary for fit with the wider system ([Anthropic, 2024](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). Evidence: vendor guidance from customer deployments, no data.

Anthropic's success-criteria and evaluation guide ranks graders: code-based grading is fastest and most reliable but lacks nuance, human grading is highest quality but slow, and LLM grading is fast and flexible but must be tested for reliability before scaling; for LLM grading it prescribes detailed rubrics, an output restricted to `correct`/`incorrect` or a numeric scale, and reasoning before the score with the reasoning then discarded ([Anthropic docs, develop-tests](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests)). The hallucination guide adds three instructions that transfer directly to a judge: permit "I don't have enough information", ground judgments in extracted quotes, and require a supporting quote per claim with retraction when none exists ([Anthropic docs, reduce-hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)). Evidence: vendor guidance.

Anthropic's agent-evaluation post is the most specific first-party statement of the layering. It separates the transcript (what the agent said and did) from the outcome (the final environment state) with the example that a flight-booking agent saying "booked" is not the outcome; the reservation row in the database is ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). It recommends deterministic graders where possible, LLM graders where necessary, human graders for validation; grading what the agent produced rather than the path it took; giving the LLM judge a way out such as returning "Unknown"; grading each rubric dimension with an isolated judge call; calibrating LLM judges against human experts; making graders resistant to bypasses; and reading transcripts because a low score can come from a grading bug (rigid numeric matching held one model at 42% on a benchmark where it scored 95% after the graders were fixed) as well as from the agent ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). It also names fail-to-pass and pass-to-pass binary tests, static analysis, and outcome verification as the code-based grader types for coding agents ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). Evidence: vendor guidance with anecdotes and one measured grading-bug case.

OpenAI's graders documentation defines four grader types, `string_check`, `text_similarity`, `score_model`, and `python`, plus a `multi` grader that combines sub-scores with a formula, and the `score_model` grader asks for a numeric `result` alongside structured reasoning `steps` and applies a `pass_threshold` in configuration rather than in the prompt ([OpenAI docs, graders](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/graders)). Its guidance: validate a model grader against human-ranked answers before use, prefer smooth scores to a pass/fail stamp, guard against grader hacking, use an LLM judge only when code falls short, and detect a hacked grader by the gap between model-grader scores and expert-human scores ([OpenAI docs, graders](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/graders)). OpenAI notes the graders API is being deprecated, so the patterns, not the endpoints, are what transfers ([OpenAI docs, graders](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/graders)). Evidence: vendor guidance.

No Google DeepMind or Google Cloud document was found that adds a practice beyond these; SAFE in Section 4 is the DeepMind contribution used here.

Practice: the verify node orders its work as the vendors do. Deterministic checks (exit codes, file presence, diff scope, schema) run first and decide alone whenever they can; a typed model judgment covers only what code cannot check, one acceptance item per call, with a rubric and `unclear` allowed; thresholds live in the helper and are recorded with each verdict; anything `unclear` or below threshold is listed for the human reviewer rather than rounded to pass or fail.

## 6. A schema guarantees shape, not truth, so structured output is the parser layer of the verifier and nothing more

OpenAI's Structured Outputs constrain decoding so the response always matches the supplied JSON Schema, with refusals surfaced as a separate `refusal` field; the same page states that outputs can still contain mistakes and that a model forced to fill a schema from unrelated input will hallucinate values, so the prompt should allow an empty or sentinel answer ([OpenAI docs, structured outputs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/structured-outputs)). Anthropic's structured outputs offer the same guarantee through constrained decoding for JSON output and strict tool inputs, and state that a refusal takes precedence over the schema and a `max_tokens` stop can leave the output incomplete ([Anthropic docs, structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). Evidence: vendor API documentation.

Guardrails AI documents validator `on_fail` actions as a small policy set: `REASK` with the failure message, deterministic `FIX`, `FILTER` a failing field, `REFRAIN`, `NOOP` (log only), `EXCEPTION`, and `FIX_REASK`, which fixes deterministically first and reasks only when the fixed value still fails ([Guardrails AI docs](https://guardrailsai.com/docs/concepts/validator_on_fail_actions)). NVIDIA NeMo Guardrails describes input, retrieval, dialog, execution, and output rails as separate stages, with execution rails applied to tool calls ([NVIDIA NeMo Guardrails docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/nemo/guardrails/latest/index.html)). Neither framework publishes evidence on verification quality; their contribution is the vocabulary of ordered, typed failure handling. Evidence: framework documentation.

Practice: every model judgment the verify node makes is requested as a typed answer (enum verdict plus probability) so parsing never fails, and the schema includes an `unclear` option and an evidence pointer field so the model is never forced to invent a verdict; a schema-valid answer is then still subject to the deterministic checks in Section 5, and a `max_tokens` or refusal stop is treated as helper failure and falls back to the code rule, matching the existing `judge.mjs` contract.

## Implications for a verify node

1. Run the checks yourself. Apply the change and execute the repository's tests, lint, build, and the plan's `### Verify` commands; the verdict comes from exit codes and output, as SWE-bench grades by running the pull request's tests ([Jimenez et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.06770)) and Anthropic separates the outcome in the environment from what the transcript says ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)).
2. Treat self-reported success as a claim. Agents believe they have succeeded when hidden tests say otherwise ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/swe-bench-sonnet)), mark features done without end-to-end tests ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)), and describe their gaming strategies while denying they would cheat ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)).
3. Fail on tampered checks. Diff test files and grading scripts against the base branch and fail on deleted or weakened tests, special-cased inputs, or hard-coded expected values, the behaviors named in the Claude 4 system card ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-4-system-card)) and METR's reports ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/evaluations/claude-3-7-report/)).
4. Keep the verifier separate from the implementer. Use a different session and, where the harness allows, a different model family, because judges recognize and favor their own output ([Panickssery et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076); [Liu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.16634)), and independent verification questions outperform ones answered with the draft in view ([Dhuliawala et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11495)).
5. Grade one acceptance item at a time against named evidence. Atomic claims with per-claim support are what FActScore and SAFE measure ([Min et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251); [Wei et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18802)), and step-level labels beat outcome-only labels ([Lightman et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.20050)); report supported items and covered items separately.
6. Deterministic checks before model judgments. Code graders are fastest and most reliable; a model grader covers only what code cannot check and must be tested before it is trusted ([Anthropic docs, develop-tests](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests); [OpenAI docs, graders](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/graders)).
7. Give the judge a rubric, a reference, and an `unclear` exit. Reference-guided grading cut judge failures from 70% to 15% ([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)); "Unknown" as an allowed answer and quote-grounded claims reduce invented verdicts ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents); [Anthropic docs, reduce-hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)).
8. Avoid pairwise grading; there is one candidate. Single-answer grading against a rubric matched human preference in Zheng's data, and position bias only arises when two answers share a prompt ([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)); if a comparison is ever needed, swap positions and count a win only when both orders agree.
9. Ask open questions, not leading ones. Ask the judge what the output shows rather than whether it confirms the claim, since yes/no framing pulls the model toward agreement ([Dhuliawala et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11495)) and longer answers pull it toward approval ([Zheng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685)).
10. Record probabilities and thresholds with every verdict. Probability-weighted scores carry more information than a single sampled label ([Liu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.16634)); `pass_threshold` belongs in configuration, not the prompt ([OpenAI docs, graders](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/graders)); the record lets a later calibration pass tune the bar, as the existing helper already does for `size-children`.
11. Route low confidence to a person, and read the transcript before trusting a failure. Model-based hack monitors were only good enough to select cases for human review ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)), and low scores were caused by grader bugs as often as by the agent in Anthropic's account ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)).
12. Spend verification where the risk is. Adaptive allocation of checking effort by difficulty beat uniform best-of-N at a quarter of the cost ([Snell et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.03314)); a plan's risk notes and the size of the diff per acceptance item are the difficulty signal available to the node, and tasks with tests of good coverage are the cheap-to-verify case Wei describes ([Wei, 2025](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law)).
